How our Liar's Dice bot works

An honest tour of shaman247/htq, the bot we built for cyberleague's Liar's Dice league — and the practice arena we built to train it.

The short version

We built a bot that plays Liar's Dice on cyberleague. To make it good, we first built a practice arena with sixteen made-up opponents, each one playing a different style. Our bot beats every single one of them — never below 60% wins, and often closer to 90%. It does this by combining three things: spotting patterns in how the opponent plays, simulating what would happen next, and falling back to plain probability math when nothing else fits.

What is Liar's Dice?

Imagine you and one friend each have a cup with five dice inside. You both roll, peek at your own dice, and hide them from each other. Now you take turns making claims about all the dice on the table — yours plus theirs, even though neither of you can see the other's.

A claim looks like "there are at least three 4s". The next player has two choices:

  • Raise the claim to something bigger — like "at least four 4s" or "at least three 5s".
  • Call liar. Everyone reveals their dice. If the claim was true, the challenger loses. If it was false, the claimer loses.

One twist: 1s are wild. They count as whatever face the current claim is about. So if the claim is "three 4s" and you have one 4 and two 1s, that counts as three 4s on your side alone.

That's the whole game. It sounds simple, but it's brutal — every bid you make leaks information about your dice, and every bid your opponent makes is a clue you can try to read.

The challenge

Cyberleague is a programming league where everyone submits a bot, and the bots play each other over and over. We don't know who the other bots are, what strategies they use, or how good they are. We just submit our bot and watch the win/loss record.

The obvious problem: how do you train a bot when you don't know who it'll be fighting?

Our answer was to build a practice arena filled with imaginary opponents. Each imaginary opponent plays a different fixed style — some careful, some reckless, some weird. If our bot beats all of them, it's probably also going to do well against the real bots out there, because the real bots are usually some mix of these styles.

The cool part: when we did lose to a real opponent on cyberleague, we'd record the game, look at how the opponent played, and add a new imaginary opponent to our arena that played the same way. Then we'd tune our bot until it beat that new opponent too. The arena grew over time, shaped by real losses.

The arena: sixteen opponent personalities

Here's everyone in our practice arena. Think of each one as a person at a poker table with a fixed, predictable personality.

random
Picks moves at random. Has no strategy at all. This is the "is our bot smarter than a coin flip" test.
always-challenge
Never bids. Calls liar on the very first chance. Easy to beat — just open with a true claim.
never-challenge
Refuses to call liar, even when the bid is clearly impossible. Just keeps raising. You beat it by waiting until the claim is too big to be real.
honest
Plays "fair" — bids what it actually expects to be true based on its own dice plus a guess at yours. Only calls liar when a bid clearly can't be true.
conservative
Only bids what it can see in its own hand. Very safe, but predictable.
aggressive
Bids high, almost never calls liar. Tries to bully you into bad challenges.
bluffer
Adds a random "puff" to every bid — a little inflation on top of what's reasonable. Calls liar only occasionally.
min-increment
Always raises by exactly one. The slow-and-steady type.
calculator
Does real probability math, picks the most-likely-to-be-true bid every time. The strongest baseline — basically a "rational" player.
minimal-safe
Picks the smallest bid that's still very likely (≥55% chance) to be true. Tight and disciplined.
copycat
Always raises by one on whatever face you just bid. Hard to fight because it shadows your moves.
aggressive-opener
Opens with a big bid to scare you off, then plays normally after. Folds on near-impossible bids.
bold-opener
Like aggressive-opener but raises only by one each time.

How our bot works

Our bot has three layers. On every turn, it walks down the layers in order and uses the first one that fits.

Layer 1

Pattern detection

Before doing any math, look at the opponent's moves so far. Does it fit a pattern we recognize?

  • "High opener that always +1's" — opponent opened with at least three of some face, and every raise since has just been "+1 on the same face". This matches bold-opener, honest, and stubborn-bold-opener.
  • "Copycat pattern" — opponent opened with quantity 2 and has been mirroring your bids with +1 raises ever since.

When a pattern matches, we know roughly what kind of opponent we're facing — which means we can simulate the rest of the game more accurately.

Layer 2

Belief framework

If we recognized a pattern, we ask a question: "Given that the opponent is probably one of these styles, and probably has roughly this many of each face in their hand — what's my best move right now?"

The bot considers three kinds of moves:

  1. Call liar right now.
  2. Raise on the same face the opponent just bid.
  3. Switch the face to something else — useful when we secretly have a lot of, say, 5s and the bidding has been about 3s.

For each option, the bot mentally plays out what would happen next — including how the opponent would respond — and picks the move with the best chance of winning. The arithmetic for this is just careful bookkeeping with probabilities; nothing too fancy.

One nice trick: if we already have four or more of some face the opponent hasn't bid about, we just bluntly switch to that face. It's almost guaranteed to be true, and it works about 87% of the time when it fires.

Layer 3

Probability fallback

If no pattern matched, we fall back to plain math. We assume nothing about the opponent's style, just that they have five hidden dice. Then:

  • Calculate the chance that the opponent's most recent bid is actually true. If it's below 40%, call liar.
  • Otherwise, pick a bid that's likely enough to be true and also better than the gamble of challenging.

This layer is unglamorous but reliable — it's the safety net that catches every weird opponent we didn't anticipate.

Every single opponent loses more often than they win — on both sides of the table. The worst single-side rate is 50% (min-increment as P2), and the average across all cells is about 75%.

The interesting pattern is in the splits: against opponents who play passively or honestly, we'd rather drive. Against opponents who overcommit (bluffer, aggressive at high stakes, never-challenge), we'd rather let them open and walk into the trap. Most of the bot's pattern-detection branches only activate when the opponent opens, which is why our "they open" numbers are higher against opponents the detectors were specifically designed for (bluffer, face-raiser, six-fixator, stubborn-bold-opener) and lower against opponents who play passively or symmetrically (aggressive, min-increment, copycat).

The algorithm, step by step

A quick note on notation: When we talk about a bid, we'll write it as (Quantity, Face). So, (3, 4) means "Three 4s".

Here's what the bot actually does on every turn, in order. It walks down a list of branches and uses the first one that fits; if nothing fits, it falls back to plain probability math.

Step 0 — Read the situation

  1. Look at the game history. If it's empty, we're opening — skip straight to the fallback (Step 5) and pick the safest opening bid.
  2. Otherwise, find the opponent's most recent bid. Call it prev = (Q, F).

Step 1 — "High opener, then +1's on the same face" (Branch 0)

Check whether the opening bid was the opponent's first move with quantity ≥ 3, and every opponent raise since has been "+1 on the same face." (If the open was exactly 3, also require at least one raise — otherwise we can't tell this apart from a one-shot aggressive opener.)

If this pattern fires and the current face is still the opener's face:

  1. Face-switch shortcut. If we hold 4 or 5 of some face F' different from the opener's, bid (max(our_count+1, Q+1), F'). We almost certainly have it; the opponent almost certainly doesn't.
  2. Belief-framework move. Otherwise, run the belief search (Step 4) over the three archetypes {AggressiveOpener, BoldOpener, Honest}, and guess how many of that face they have based on standard odds for someone playing their "favorite" number.

Step 2 — Copycat pattern (Branch 1)

Check: did the opponent open with quantity exactly 2, and has every raise been "+1 on the same face"? If so, run the belief search (Step 4) looking specifically at the Copycat style.

Step 3 — We opened, opponent has been +1-ing (Branch 3)

Check: did we make the first bid, has every opponent move been "+1 on the same face" since, and is the current face ≥ 2? If so, we test our theory against seven possible opponent styles {AggressiveOpener, BoldOpener, Honest, MinIncrement, Copycat, Calculator, MinimalSafe}. We assume they have an average, mathematically normal hand of dice. If no move gives us better than a 50% chance of winning, we don't trust our guess and drop down to the math fallback (Step 5).

Step 4 — The belief search (used by branches 0, 1, and 3)

This is the engine. Given a list of suspected opponent styles and a guess about what's in their hand:

  1. Guess their hand: We look at the opponent styles we suspect we're facing, and guess what dice they hold based on standard odds.
  2. Cross off the impossibilities: We look at our past bids. If the opponent didn't call "liar" on us, we eliminate any opponent style or dice combo that would have called liar in that situation.
  3. Test "Call Liar": We calculate the raw odds that their current bid is already a lie based on the dice we can see on our side of the table.
  4. Simulate raising the bid: If we raise the quantity, what happens? We play out the rest of the game in our head (a few moves into the future) against the remaining suspected opponent types to see if we end up winning the showdown.
  5. Simulate changing the face: We do the exact same future-simulation, but we try switching the topic to a different number (like changing the bid to 5s).
  6. Pick the winner: We choose the move — call liar, raise, or switch — that our simulation says wins the most often.

Step 5 — Plain-probability fallback (v3)

If no branch fired, ignore the opponent's identity and reason from raw probabilities:

  1. Calculate the odds: Calculate the raw odds that their bid is actually true. If they are bidding on a specific face, we assume there's a 75% chance it's their "favorite" face (meaning they probably hold a lot of them) and a 25% chance it's just a normal, random hand.
  2. If that probability is below 40%, call liar.
  3. Otherwise, look at all legal next bids and pick the one with the highest probability of being true (if there's a tie, go with the smaller bid).
  4. Sanity check: if even our best possible bid is much worse than just challenging right now, we challenge instead.
Why the layered structure? The belief framework is powerful but only useful when we can actually identify the opponent. The plain-probability fallback is weaker on average but never makes confident-but-wrong assumptions. Layering them gives us the best of both — sharp play when we have a read, safe play when we don't.

How the bot got to this point

The bot went through eleven versions. The interesting jumps:

v1 → v3: the "best face" insight

Version 1 just did straight probability math. It worked OK but lost badly to one opponent — never-challenge, which always bids on its own best face. The problem: when an opponent bids on a face, that face being their best is itself a clue. If they're bidding on 4s, they probably have a lot of 4s, even if you mathematically wouldn't expect them to.

Version 3 fixed this by precomputing, for every possible "best face", the distribution of how many of that face the opponent likely has. This jumped our win rate from 62% to 75% in the original tournament.

v4: more math, worse result

Version 4 was a more "principled" probability calculation that should have been better. It wasn't. It got steamrolled by copycat because the new math kept saying "your bid is still probably true, keep raising" — and copycat would just keep mirroring until v4 ran out of safe raises.

The lesson: being more correct in theory only helps if your opponents play the way your theory assumes. In practice, the opponent's quirks matter more than mathematical purity. We rolled back to v3 and built on top of it instead.

v11: pattern detection on top of v3

The current version keeps the v3 probability core as a safety net, but adds the pattern-detection and belief-framework layers on top. The big breakthrough was treating face-switching (changing the subject from 4s to 5s, say) as a first-class option. Previous versions only thought about "raise or call liar". Adding face-switching cracked the copycat matchup — we used to think 51-53% was the theoretical ceiling there, and it turned out to be 64%.

Things we tried that didn't work

For honesty's sake, here are the experiments that flopped:

  • Predicting which face the opponent will switch to next. We tried giving each opponent style a "what would you bid next" model. The model was too crude — to predict the actual next bid, you'd need to know their full hand, which we don't. Result: a catastrophic regression from 60% to 33%.
  • Tightening the "should I keep raising" rule. Made the bot fold sooner on borderline bids. Won a few extra games against honest players, lost many more against aggressive ones. Net negative, reverted.
  • Lowering the face-switch trigger from "I have 4+" down to "I have 3+". Sounded reasonable, was a disaster against honest — win rate crashed from 55% to 40%.

The common thread: every "improvement" that assumed too much about the opponent's style backfired on opponents who didn't fit. The bot we shipped is the version that knows when not to be clever.

The bigger lesson

The thing we found ourselves doing over and over: look at where we lost, copy the winner's style into our arena, and tune the bot until it stops losing to that style. It's not glamorous — there's no single brilliant algorithm at the heart of the bot. It's just a stack of small, specific responses to real opponents, plus a careful math fallback for everything else.

If there's a takeaway for anyone building competitive bots: real opponents are weirder than synthetic ones, and a practice arena is only as good as how quickly you can grow it after each loss.
